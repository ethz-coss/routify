# Street-type hierarchy and noise exposure — findings

Interpretation of the street-type/noise analysis. The numbers themselves are
in two CSVs, which are the record of the result:

| file | contents |
|---|---|
| `noise_hierarchy_by_street_type.csv` | per-class edge counts, length, and noise statistics (arithmetic, energy-domain, median, sd, min/max, length- and usage-weighted), plus each class's drive multiplier, importance rank and missing-data rate |
| `noise_hierarchy_correlations.csv` | the four correlation coefficients, with n and a note on each |

Both are regenerated by `NoiseHierarchyAnalysis.py`. This document contains no
data that is not in those files; it argues what the data means.

---

## Method, and what the sample is

Per-edge noise is not exposed by the graph export (`/status/graph/` carries
`source, target, distance, slope, highway` only), so the routing output is the
only source for it. The analysis reads all 80 batch files of the
`routing_mode_noise` sweep.

Noise is a **static graph attribute** — `CustomEdge` assigns each edge the mean
of its two vertices' raster values — so edges are de-duplicated by edge id and
each physical edge contributes exactly one observation, regardless of how often
the router traversed it. Weighting by traversal count would measure the
router's preferences rather than the network's properties. The usage-weighted
column is retained in the CSV for anyone who wants that second question, but
none of the conclusions below rest on it.

This yields 221 701 unique edges from 14 645 919 traversals — **50.6% of the
graph's 438 457 edges**.

Two limits on the sample matter:

- **It is route-driven, not a census.** Only edges some route actually used
  appear. `footway` is 52% of the sampled edges because walk routes dominate
  the three transport modes. Per-class statistics are sound; the marginal
  distribution *across* classes is not representative of the graph.
- **0.7% of edges carry `noise = 0`**, i.e. missing rather than silent. These
  are excluded. They are concentrated in `pedestrian` (4.8%), `track` (4.5%)
  and `path` (2.7%) and absent from every road class — so the exclusion acts
  precisely on the quiet end and could have biased the result. It does not:
  including them moves every correlation by less than 0.01. The finding is
  robust to the choice, which is why the missing-data rate is carried per class
  in the CSV rather than silently dropped.

---

## 1. The hierarchy predicts noise, strongly and monotonically

Across the drivable network, noise falls as road class descends — from
`primary` at 71.1 dB to `track` at 39.3 dB, a **31.8 dB span**.

| relationship | n | Spearman ρ |
|---|---|---|
| importance rank vs noise, edge level | 63 387 | −0.670 |
| class mean noise vs importance rank | 14 classes | −0.729 |

The sign is negative because rank 1 is `motorway`: higher in the hierarchy
means louder. Spearman is the appropriate coefficient here — importance rank is
ordinal, and the relationship is monotone but not linear.

This is the expected result and it is worth stating as a validation rather than
a discovery: it confirms the noise raster is coupled to the road network in the
way it should be, which is a precondition for anything else in this document.

**One anomaly.** `trunk` sits at 58.5 dB, well below its neighbours and against
the trend. n = 80 edges over 4 km — too small to weigh, and most likely
tunnelled or peripheral segments. It should not be read as a finding.

---

## 2. The routing cost hierarchy is a poor proxy for the noise hierarchy

Correlating noise against the *drive cost multiplier* instead of road class
gives ρ = −0.672 over eligible classes, which looks equivalent. It is not, and
using it that way would be a mistake.

Eleven of the fourteen drive-eligible classes collapse to the same multiplier of
×1.0. Within that single tier, noise ranges from **58.5 to 71.1 dB**. The cost
hierarchy has three distinct levels; the noise variation is almost entirely
*inside* the largest of them, where the cost function cannot see it.

The apparent correlation is carried by the three classes that do differ
(`residential` ×1.5, `living_street` ×2.0, `service` ×2.5) all being quiet. That
is a real association, but it is coincidental to the cost design rather than
expressed by it — the multipliers encode drivability preference, not exposure.

The practical consequence: **road class is a usable predictor of noise; the
routing weight is not.** Any analysis that wants to reason about noise from the
cost structure should use `highway` directly.

---

## 3. The pedestrian and cycle network is not the quiet network

This is the finding that should change how the walk and cycle results are read.

`footway` averages **58.8 dB — 3.6 dB louder than `residential`** (55.1), and
`cycleway` reaches **64.7 dB**, louder than every non-arterial road class and
within 3 dB of `tertiary`.

The mechanism is straightforward once the noise assignment is examined: the
value comes from the raster at the edge's two vertices, so a sidewalk running
beside an arterial inherits that arterial's exposure. Segregated cycle lanes
along main roads inherit it too. The infrastructure is separated in the graph
but not in the acoustic field.

The genuinely quiet infrastructure is `track` (39.3 dB) and `path` (44.8 dB) —
and both sit in the ×10 000 tier, formally excluded for cars. A car route that
touches them, as roughly 4.9% of drive-route length does because
`FilterDisallowedOperation` is disabled and the penalty is finite rather than
infinite, is also traversing its quietest segments.

For the sensitivity analysis this means the noise slider is not choosing
between "roads" and "pedestrian infrastructure". It is choosing between
*corridors*: arterial corridors carry their footways and cycleways with them,
and the quiet alternatives are the off-network classes.

---

## 4. Linear dB averaging understates exposure, unevenly

`PostProcessDataFull.py` reduces each route's noise to a length-weighted
**arithmetic** mean of dB values. Decibels are logarithmic, so this has no
physical interpretation; exposure must be averaged in the energy domain,
`10·log10(mean(10^(L/10)))`.

The two disagree in proportion to within-class variance, and the gap is largest
exactly where the noise-optimised routes go:

| class | arithmetic | energy | gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| `track` | 39.3 | 55.8 | **+16.5** |
| `path` | 44.8 | 61.5 | **+16.6** |
| `corridor` | 39.8 | 56.3 | +16.5 |
| `steps` | 49.4 | 60.2 | +10.8 |
| `residential` | 55.1 | 61.2 | +6.1 |
| `primary` | 71.1 | 72.0 | +0.9 |

Homogeneous arterials barely move. Heterogeneous quiet classes move by more
than 16 dB.

This is not a rounding concern. A noise-optimised route lowers its reported
score by shifting onto `track`, `path` and similar classes — precisely the
classes where the arithmetic mean most understates true energy exposure. **The
measured benefit of noise-aware routing is therefore inflated by the metric
itself, and inflated most in the routes that appear to benefit most.**

Recommendation: recompute the reduced `noise` column in the energy domain
before quantifying any noise-reduction claim. The per-class energy figures are
already in the CSV; the change to the reduction is one line. The route-level
recomputation needs the raw sweep output, since the reduced files retain only
the already-averaged value.

## Reproducing

```bash
cd jupyter
python3 NoiseHierarchyAnalysis.py \
    --in /mnt/data/routify-sweep/results_splitted/routing_mode_noise
```

First run scans the sweep (~5 min) and caches per-edge values to
`/mnt/data/routify-sweep/logs/noise_edges.csv`; later runs reuse the cache and
finish in seconds. `--rescan` forces a re-read, `--outdir` redirects the CSVs.
The per-edge cache is 12 MB and deliberately kept out of the repository.

Constants at the top of the script — `DRIVE_MULT` and `IMPORTANCE` — mirror
`config_features.json` and `RoutifyConfig.allowedFeaturesDrive`. If either
changes in the backend, update them to match.
